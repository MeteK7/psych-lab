# CS3 "Not enough to go on": redesign of steps 3 and 4 (revision 2)

> **Status:** revised proposal, 2026-11-03, **for your review.** Nothing is built.
> - Revision 2 replaces revision 1 after a second, critical review of measurement, readings, starting points, step 4 and the evidence.
> - What changed from revision 1, and why, is in §6.

## 1. What the exercise should teach
**Goal:** after one visit, the person can use two questions on their own, in a moment like this:
1. **"Would what happened also fit another reading?"** If it would, what happened doesn't tell me which reading is right. My certainty came from my reading, not from the facts.
2. **"What would actually tell me, and is it worth finding out?"** Imagining possibilities isn't information. Sometimes I can get information; sometimes the honest answer is "I don't know".

**Not the goal:**
- More readings for their own sake.
- A more positive reading.
- Lower certainty as a sign of success: someone whose first reading was right should be able to keep it.

**What one visit can't do:** nothing here shows that one visit changes how people think next time. Transfer is the main open question (§7). The design does what it can to support it:
- the person makes every judgement themselves
- the conclusion is stated plainly
- the two questions are handed over at the end, to take away

## 2. Step 3: why the 10 points have to go
**The comparison isn't valid.** Revision 1 compared step 2 certainty (0–10) with step 3 points (0–10) and presented the gap as a shift. The two measures aren't comparable:
- **Different questions:**
  - Step 2 asks for **confidence in one reading, judged alone**, against an unstated "anything else".
  - Step 3 asks for **shares of a fixed total over a visible list**, so every point given to another reading is taken from the first.
- **Listing alternatives changes judgements by itself.** I read the full text of Redelmeier, Koehler, Liberman & Tversky (1995), which tests support theory with physicians:
  - **Adding listed diagnoses:** adding three named diagnoses to the list raised the share given to "everything other than the first two" from 50% to 69%, and halved the estimate for one listed diagnosis (31% → 16%). Nothing about the patient had changed.
  - **Judging one option alone:** physicians judging one prognosis each gave estimates that summed to 164%. Each listed possibility gets more weight than when it's left unnamed.
  - **Two options:** with just two options, judgements were consistent (78% + 21% = 99%). That is exactly the situation of step 2, one reading against "not this one".
- **The number of options pulls estimates towards an even split** (Fox & Rottenstreich 2003). An independent registered replication confirmed this, with smaller effects than the original (Ding & Feldman 2025: N = 603, effects r ≈ 0.1–0.3, two of the original effects not replicated).

**How much of a "gap" the format alone produces** can't be stated for our exact screen. But the direction is clear, and the size can be large: the format alone halved one estimate in the medical study. With 7 rows, someone who doesn't change their mind at all would still be expected to give their first reading less than they said in step 2. **So the gap can't show that a belief changed,** and showing it as if it did would be misleading.

**Points also fail on two other counts:**
- **No learning:** sharing points practises estimating, not checking evidence. Estimating "how likely each seems" is still imagining.
- **Not neutral:** for someone whose first reading was benign and right, the format still drags it down.

**What replaces them:** a judgement the person makes about each reading, whose logical consequence can be stated honestly (§4, step 3).

## 3. Readings: rules instead of a fixed template
Revision 1 forced one reading of each of six kinds per scenario. Checked reading by reading, that produced overlaps. "They read it at a bad moment and forgot" and "They'll reply when they get to it; it means nothing" predict the same thing. "Nothing to read into" mostly duplicated "about the situation".

**New rules for every scenario set (4–5 readings, plus "Something I haven't thought of"):**

| Rule | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Plausible** | Reviewers rate each reading ≥ 4 of 5 (existing rule). The plausibility of the alternative is what makes considering it work: Hirt & Markman 1995 found plausibility moderated debiasing. |
| **Distinct** | Two readings may stay only if finding out could tell them apart. Reviewers check this, and they check it pair by pair. |
| **Coverage, not quotas** | The set includes the feared reading about me; a milder, fixable one about me; and at least two that aren't about me (the other person's state or habits, or the circumstances). A positive reading appears only where one is realistic. |
| **Same level of detail** | Negative and benign readings are equally specific, so neither is favoured by being spelled out. The physician study recommends this as a correction for exactly this effect, but didn't test it. |
| **Honest labels** | Readings get a *kind* for authors and reviewers (about me, mild, about them, circumstances, positive). They get no "valence" score, and no balance count that the code relies on. |

**Revised sets** (the feared reading first here for clarity; on screen, a fixed mixed order):
1. **Teacher: "Come and see me after class"**
   - "It's bad news about my grade." · "There's something I need to fix before it's final." · "They'd rather explain feedback in person than in writing." · "They're asking several students to come by." · "They liked something and want to talk about it."
2. **Friend hasn't replied for a day**
   - "They don't want to talk to me." · "Something I said bothered them a bit." · "They've got a lot going on right now." · "They saw it at a bad moment and forgot." · "They want to reply properly when they have time."
3. **Manager moves the weekly meeting**
   - "Something bad is coming for me." · "My project isn't their priority this week." · "They're under pressure from above." · "Something else had to go in that slot." · "They want a time when they can give it proper attention."
4. **Group chat: no reactions**
   - "They think it's a bad idea but don't want to say so." · "It wasn't clear what I was suggesting." · "It got buried and people haven't really read it." · "Everyone's waiting for someone else to answer." · "Some people agree but didn't think to react."
5. **Essay: "Interesting, but is this the right question?"**
   - "The whole essay misses the point." · "My question needs sharpening." · "That's how this teacher pushes everyone." · "They had a lot to mark, so the comment is short." · "They found it interesting enough to challenge."
6. **Classmate walks past without saying hello**
   - "They don't like me." · "They're a bit annoyed with me about something." · "They didn't see me." · "They were caught up in their own thoughts." · "They felt awkward and weren't sure whether to say hi."
   - **No positive reading here,** because none would be realistic.

**Own situation:** four general readings to tick, plus the person's own:
- "It means what I fear it means."
- "There's something small I could fix."
- "It's about something going on with them."
- "It's about the circumstances, not about me."

## 4. The revised exercise: still 5 steps, about 4 minutes
For each step: **what the person practises**, **how that could produce learning**, and **what they should be able to do afterwards**. None of it is assumed to follow automatically from finishing the step.

| Step | On screen | Practises | How it could produce learning | Afterwards |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. An unclear situation** | Unchanged: pick a scenario or describe your own | Choosing a concrete moment | A skill is learned on a concrete case. The own-situation route lets it be the person's real case | — (set-up) |
| **2. Your first reading** | Unchanged: pick or write the first reading. "How sure are you of this reading?" 0–10 | Naming the automatic reading **as a reading**, separate from what happened | Labelling an interpretation makes it something to examine rather than a fact | Can say "that's how I read it", not "that's what it was" |
| **3. Could these explain it?** (replaces the points) | Shows what happened, and the person's reading. **"Which of these could also explain what happened? Tick any that could."** Lists the scenario's other readings, plus "Something I haven't thought of". One optional own reading: "Is there a reason that's about them or the circumstances, not about you?" Then a short note, stated from the person's own ticks (below) | Checking for each alternative whether **the same facts fit it**. This is the core reasoning step: evidence that fits several readings doesn't pick one out | The person makes each judgement themselves, and the note states only the logical consequence of those judgements. It's the "consider an alternative" strategy (Lord et al. 1984; Hirt & Markman 1995), made concrete: few plausible alternatives (Sanna et al. 2002), ready-made rather than generated from scratch | Can ask "Would this also have happened if…?" |
| **4. Can you find out?** | Opens: "Thinking it through tells you what's possible, not what happened." A line to help decide: "It's worth finding out if it matters to you, and finding out wouldn't cost more than it's worth (time, awkwardness)." One choice, with a short follow-up on the same screen (below) | Separating possibilities from information, and making a deliberate choice: find out, or knowingly leave it open | The choice is the person's own, with the deciding factors named. Getting information is what actually increased accuracy in Eyal et al. 2018. Leaving it open is presented as a legitimate result, not a failure | Can ask "What would actually tell me, and is it worth it?" |
| **5. How sure are you now?** | The same 0–10 question as step 2, with no judgement of the answer. Then the two questions to keep (§1), and an optional "Plan it as an if-then step" if the person chose to ask or check | Noticing without being graded; taking away the two questions | The questions are named explicitly at the end, so they can be recalled next time. Whether people actually use them later is untested (§7) | Has two questions to use next time |

**Step 3 note, written by rules from the ticks:**
- **One or more ticked:** "What happened could fit {n} readings, including yours. On its own, it doesn't show which is right."
- **None ticked:** "You think only your reading fits what happened. If you know more than this moment, it may well be right. If you don't, the others may deserve a second look."
- **Always:** "These are possibilities, not information."

The note never says the person changed their mind. It reports only what they judged.

**Step 4, the choice and its follow-up:**

| Choice | Follow-up on the same screen |
|---|---|
| **I'll ask them** | A suggestion, not a rule: "One way to ask without assuming your reading: ‘I wasn't sure what you meant. Could you tell me?’" |
| **I'll check something myself** | — |
| **I'll wait. It'll probably become clear** | "If it's still on your mind later and nothing's changed, the other choices are still there." |
| **I'll leave it. Finding out isn't possible or isn't worth it** | "Then the honest answer is: you don't know. That's a real answer, not a failure to work it out." |

**Recorded:**
- step 3: ids of the ticked readings, and whether an own reading was added (never its text)
- step 4: the choice id
- the two certainty ratings, as now

### 4.1 When the first reading isn't negative
The exercise is offered for "assuming the worst", but people won't always start there. With the revised design:
- **Steps 3 and 4 are neutral about valence.** "Would what happened also fit another reading?" and "What would tell me?" are as useful when the first reading is "They're just busy" as when it's "They hate me". Nothing pushes towards any particular reading.
- **No success direction.** Lower certainty isn't treated as better; the certainty in step 5 isn't judged. Revision 1's closing line ("Holding more than one reading open isn't indecision") implied that it was, and is dropped.
- **A benign first reading can be the right one.** If someone ticks the feared reading as possible, step 4 lets them decide that it isn't worth finding out. That is a legitimate end, so the exercise doesn't invent a worry.
- **A first reading backed by more than this moment** (for example, an argument yesterday) is acknowledged in the step 3 note ("If you know more than this moment, it may well be right").

- **Cited directly:** Eyal 2018 is the only finding that directly supports a claim the screen makes, alongside the current interpretation-training findings (Hallion, Cristea).
- **Stays as design rationale:** the rest remains in this document and isn't cited on screen. The full texts of Lord, Hirt & Markman, Sanna and Koriat would be needed before any of them is cited; you could get them from a library.

## 7. Remaining uncertainties
1. **No direct evidence** that this exercise, or any single short visit like it, produces learning that people use later. The design rests on related findings and on reasoning about evidence.
2. **The tick list is also a list.** Listing readings makes ticking them more likely (support theory). The step 3 note stays honest because it reports the person's own judgements, but how many they tick is partly shaped by the format.
3. **The before/after certainty** is a self-report after an exercise that invites doubt. Any change can't be read as proof of learning. It's for the person to notice, not for the app to judge.
4. **Ticking "could this explain it?" may feel obvious** for some scenarios. A walkthrough with reviewers would show this.
5. **Distinctness and plausibility** of the new readings need the content review. Wording will change.
6. **Several sources were read from abstracts only** (§5). They inform the design but aren't cited on screen until read in full.

**How to check learning without new research:** add one question to the review walkthrough: "In your own words, what did this exercise teach you?" If reviewers can't name something close to the two questions, the design isn't achieving its goal.

## 8. Decisions for you
1. **Replace the 10 points with "Could these explain it?"** (§2, §4), or keep points as a separate likelihood step with no comparison to step 2.
2. **Readings by rules** (plausible, distinct, coverage, same detail; 4–5 per scenario) instead of six fixed kinds, including no positive reading where none is realistic (§3).
3. **One "Can you find out?" step** with a short follow-up, the "ask" example as a suggestion, and no timing rules (§4).
4. **No judgement of the certainty change; two questions to take away at the end** (§4, §4.1).
5. **Add the "what did it teach you?" question** to the review walkthrough (§7).
6. **Obtain the full texts** of Lord 1984, Hirt & Markman 1995, Sanna 2002 and Koriat 1980, if you want any of them cited on screen.
